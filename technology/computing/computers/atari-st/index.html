16-BIT GENERATION / FAMILY 08

Atari ST:
the music and desktop machine.

Atari's ST family arrived in 1985 with a Motorola 68000 processor, a graphical desktop, generous memory for the price and built-in MIDI ports. It became a serious rival to the Amiga, a popular European home and business computer, and an unlikely studio standard for musicians.

One family, several directions

The ST range began as an affordable all-round personal computer, then expanded into machines for home users, musicians, offices and technical work. The later STE improved graphics and sound, while the TT030 and Falcon030 moved into more powerful 32-bit territory.

The ST architecture

Motorola 68000

The original ST used an 8 MHz Motorola 68000. Like the Amiga, it is often described as a 16/32-bit processor: it has 32-bit registers and internal architecture, but a 16-bit external data bus.

Graphics and display

The original ST offered 320 × 200 with 16 colours from a palette of 512, 640 × 200 with four colours, and 640 × 400 monochrome. The high-resolution mode paired especially well with Atari's monochrome monitor and made the ST useful for word processing and desktop publishing.

Sound

The Yamaha YM2149 sound generator provided three tone channels and noise. It was capable, but not the sampled-audio powerhouse associated with the Amiga. The STE later added stereo DMA sample playback.

TOS and GEM

The ST ran TOS (The Operating System), with GEM (Graphics Environment Manager) providing windows, icons, menus and a mouse-driven desktop. Early 520ST machines initially loaded part of TOS from floppy disk; later models stored it in ROM.

From 520ST to 1040ST

520ST · 1985

The first ST models appeared in 1985. The 520ST offered 512 KB of RAM, a keyboard-integrated case and external disk and display options. Atari's aggressive pricing made it a direct competitor to the Amiga 1000.

1040ST · 1986

The 1040ST doubled memory to 1 MB and integrated the power supply and double-sided floppy drive into the case. Its value was striking: the model name highlighted the one-megabyte configuration. The ST became particularly popular in Europe.

Mega ST · 1987

The Mega models used a separate keyboard and a more expandable desktop case, with configurations of 1, 2 or 4 MB. They appealed to professional users and studios, and some included a Blitter chip to accelerate graphics operations.

STE, TT and Falcon: the later machines

520STE and 1040STE · 1989

The STE models enhanced the original design with a 4,096-colour palette, hardware scrolling, a Blitter and stereo sampled sound. Some older software that accessed hardware directly did not behave perfectly on the newer machines.

TT030 · 1990

The TT moved up to a 32 MHz 68030 processor and offered more advanced display options, expansion and memory configurations. It was a workstation-class computer rather than simply a faster home ST.

Falcon030 · 1992

The Falcon used a 16 MHz 68030 and added the Motorola 56001 digital signal processor, advanced video hardware and 16-bit stereo audio. It was a powerful machine for digital audio and multimedia work, but arrived late in Atari's computer business.

Why musicians chose the ST

The feature that most clearly distinguished the ST was not its graphics or sound chip: it was the pair of built-in MIDI ports. MIDI In and Out made it easy to connect keyboards, synthesisers, drum machines and sequencers without buying an add-on interface. Software such as Steinberg Pro 24 and Cubase helped turn the ST into a familiar centrepiece of affordable music studios. Its precise timing and straightforward connectivity mattered more than flashy audio effects.

Games, graphics and everyday work

  • Games: the ST had a large games library and many titles shared releases with the Amiga, though the two machines could look and sound different.
  • Music production: MIDI sequencing and notation software made it a studio tool, not just a games computer.
  • Desktop publishing: high-resolution monochrome display, GEM and applications such as Calamus made the ST useful for page layout.
  • Programming: BASIC, C, assembler and development tools supported a lively software scene.

Strengths, compromises and legacy

The ST's price, GEM desktop and MIDI connectivity gave it a distinctive identity. Its original sound hardware was more modest than the Amiga's, and graphics modes depended heavily on the monitor used. Atari's later models became increasingly capable but arrived in a market changing rapidly around IBM-compatible PCs and new consoles. The ST remains especially fondly remembered by musicians and European users.

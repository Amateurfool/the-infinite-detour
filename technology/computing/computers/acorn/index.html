8-BIT GENERATION / FAMILY 03

Acorn:
built to teach, made to explore.

Acorn's 8-bit computers became closely associated with British education, but their story reaches well beyond the classroom. From the expandable Atom to the BBC Micro and its successors, Acorn built a family of machines that encouraged programming, experimentation and a long relationship between hardware and software.

From a small Cambridge company to the BBC Micro

Acorn Atom · 1980

The Atom was a modular, expandable 6502-based computer. It could be bought as a kit or ready-built and was designed to grow through expansion boards and memory upgrades. It was a capable machine for hobbyists who wanted to understand and extend their computer rather than simply use it.

BBC Micro Model A and B · 1981

Developed in response to the BBC's Computer Literacy Project, the BBC Micro was designed as a robust, expandable educational computer. Both models used a 6502A processor at 2 MHz; the Model B offered more standard memory and interfaces than the Model A. Its unusually capable graphics, sound, expansion options and well-regarded BBC BASIC made it useful well beyond schools.

Acorn Electron · 1983

The Electron brought much of the BBC Micro experience into a lower-cost compact machine. It used a 6502 at 2 MHz and shared much of the BBC BASIC environment, but had fewer built-in interfaces and some graphics limitations. Expansion units could add capabilities, though it could not become a complete Model B.

BBC Master family · 1986

The Master 128 updated the BBC Micro design with 128 KB of total RAM in multiple banks, a numeric keypad, cartridge slots, improved ROM facilities and disk support. The family included variants such as the Master Turbo, Master ET, Master 512 and Master Compact, aimed at different uses and expansion paths.

The BBC Micro: a platform for learning

The BBC Micro's influence was amplified by the BBC's television programmes, books and software, and by its adoption in schools. It was not merely a machine that happened to be used for education: its design made expansion, interfacing and programming central to the experience. The expansion bus, user port, analogue inputs and serial interfaces made it attractive for experiments, control projects and specialist applications.

Model A, Model B and the later branches

The original Model A was the more affordable configuration, while the Model B included additional RAM and interfaces that became important for many applications. Acorn later introduced the B+ with more memory, then the Master family as a substantial refinement. The Master 128 was the best-known model, but the range also included machines tailored for networking, Turbo co-processors, 512 KB DOS compatibility and compact all-in-one use. The family is best understood as a platform with several configurations, not a single unchanging BBC Micro.

BBC BASIC and the software culture

BBC BASIC became one of the best-known features of the range. Its structured programming facilities, speed and well-designed language environment made it popular with learners and experienced programmers alike. Acorn's operating system and sideways ROM system allowed extra languages, utilities and applications to be added without replacing the core machine.

What Acorn made possible

  • Education: a consistent and capable platform for computing lessons across many British schools.
  • Experimentation: hardware interfaces and expansion options encouraged electronics, robotics and control projects.
  • Software development: BBC BASIC and the ROM system supported a rich ecosystem of educational, business and creative programs.
  • A bridge to the future: Acorn's later Archimedes line moved to its own ARM RISC architecture, a major change from the 8-bit era.

Legacy

The BBC Micro and Master became reference points in British computing. They helped a generation learn to program, supported technical education and laid cultural groundwork for Acorn's later ARM and RISC OS work. The Atom and Electron are important parts of the same story: one as an expandable enthusiast machine, the other as a more accessible companion to the BBC Micro.

16-BIT GENERATION / FAMILY 07

Commodore Amiga:
a different kind of computer.

The Amiga arrived with an ambitious idea: a personal computer built around custom chips for graphics, sound and memory movement, working alongside a Motorola processor. It could animate, play sampled sound and run several tasks at once — capabilities that made it feel startlingly different from many home computers of the mid-1980s.

A family that kept changing shape

From the desktop A1000 to the keyboard-integrated A500, expandable A2000 and later A1200, the Amiga name covered several kinds of machine. Shared ideas mattered as much as case design: custom hardware, a responsive graphical environment and a strong culture of experimentation.

The custom-chip idea

A 68000 at the centre

The early Amigas used Motorola's 68000, with a 32-bit internal architecture and a 16-bit external data bus. It is often described as a 16/32-bit processor. The Amiga belonged to the 16-bit generation in the market, but its processor design was not simply a conventional 16-bit CPU.

Agnus, Denise and Paula

The original chipset divided work between custom components. Agnus handled memory access and display timing, Denise generated video, and Paula supported audio playback and input/output. Direct memory access let these chips move data without requiring the main CPU to handle every operation.

Graphics and sound

The original chipset supported hardware sprites and a palette of up to 4,096 colours, with Hold-And-Modify enabling richer images. Paula provided four independent 8-bit audio channels. This combination was especially attractive for animation, games, music and multimedia.

Multitasking from the start

AmigaOS combined a pre-emptive multitasking kernel with graphics, sound and input services. Workbench provided a graphical desktop, while the CLI offered a command-line environment.

From A1000 to A500 and A2000

Amiga 1000 · 1985

The A1000 introduced the platform in a desktop form with a separate keyboard. It established the custom-chip architecture and AmigaOS. Its original 256 KB of Chip RAM was expandable, and the machine had a more premium positioning than later mass-market models.

Amiga 500 · 1987

The A500 put the computer and keyboard in one case, making it a natural successor to the C64 in shops and homes. It typically shipped with 512 KB of Chip RAM and became the best-known and best-selling Amiga model, especially in Europe.

Amiga 2000 · 1987

The A2000 used a desktop case with internal expansion slots. Its Zorro architecture made it adaptable for hard disks, memory, graphics and specialist video hardware, suiting users who wanted a system that could grow into production or business work.

The later generation

The A3000 moved the family towards a workstation design, using a 68030 processor and improved expansion. The A600 offered a smaller entry point, while the A1200 brought the Advanced Graphics Architecture (AGA) chipset, a 68EC020 processor and 2 MB of Chip RAM in 1992. The A4000 extended the high-end range, and the CD32 carried Amiga-derived hardware into a games console.

What people did with an Amiga

  • Games: smooth scrolling, colourful graphics and sampled sound made it a major games platform.
  • Music: trackers and sample-based tools turned the four-channel audio hardware into a creative instrument.
  • Video and animation: genlock interfaces, frame grabbers and Deluxe Paint made the Amiga useful in television graphics and desktop video.
  • Publishing and design: desktop publishing, illustration and presentation software gave it a place in studios and small organisations.
  • Programming: BASIC, C and assembler encouraged experimentation and a strong enthusiast scene.

Strengths, compromises and legacy

The Amiga's strengths came from custom hardware and coherent system design. Its limitations included memory constraints on early models, model-specific compatibility differences and the cost of expansion. As the wider PC market evolved, Commodore struggled to keep the platform competitive. The company entered bankruptcy in 1994, but the Amiga community, software and hardware ideas continued long afterwards.

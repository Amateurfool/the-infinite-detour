8-BIT GENERATION / FAMILY 04

Beyond the familiar:
other 8-bit worlds.

Sinclair, Commodore and Acorn loom large in the British home-computer story, but they were never the whole picture. Other machines offered different processors, graphics, sound and ideas about what a home computer could be. Some were designed in Britain; others arrived from the United States and found their own place in the wider market.

ERA1979–1984
PROCESSORS6502 / 6809
UK MAKERSDragon · Oric
INTERNATIONALAtari · Apple

Four very different branches of 8-bit computing

These photographs show a selection of the machines in this wider landscape. The Atari range developed as a long-running platform; Dragon and Oric were British contenders; and Apple's II family had already established a major international presence.

Atari 400, 800 and XL: a platform built around custom chips

Atari 400 and 800 · 1979

Atari's first home computers grew out of the company's experience with arcade games and consoles. Both machines used a MOS 6502 processor and a set of custom chips: ANTIC handled display-list processing, CTIA (later GTIA) helped generate colour and player-missile graphics, and POKEY provided sound, keyboard scanning and serial I/O. The 400 was the lower-cost model with a membrane keyboard; the 800 had a more conventional keyboard, a stronger expansion design and a shielded internal layout.

1200XL, 600XL and 800XL · 1983

The XL generation refined the design and brought BASIC into the machine rather than requiring a cartridge on the 600XL and 800XL. The 800XL, with 64 KB of RAM, became the best-known member of the range in many markets. Later XE models extended the family, including the 130XE with 128 KB. Compatibility across generations was generally strong, though operating-system revisions and hardware changes could trip up some older software.

Why the Atari architecture stood out

The custom display and sound chips gave developers tools that were unusual for the period. The SIO serial bus also made it possible to connect peripherals in a chain, including cassette recorders, disk drives and printers. The result was a coherent family rather than a succession of unrelated models.

Dragon 32 and 64: a Welsh computer with a powerful heart

Dragon 32 · 1982

Designed by PA Consulting and manufactured by Dragon Data in Port Talbot, Wales, the Dragon 32 used a Motorola MC6809E processor at about 0.89 MHz and included 32 KB of RAM. It shared much of its underlying design with the Tandy TRS-80 Color Computer, including Microsoft Extended Colour BASIC, which gave it a useful software connection beyond the UK.

Dragon 64 · 1983

The Dragon 64 doubled memory to 64 KB and added an RS-232 serial port. Its extra memory also made it more suitable for operating systems such as OS-9, which could turn the machine towards a more sophisticated, multi-user style of computing. The 32 and 64 looked very similar, with case colour and rear connections helping distinguish them.

Strengths and limitations

The 6809E was a capable and elegant 8-bit processor, and the Dragon offered a good BASIC environment and useful expansion options. Its graphics hardware, however, was less flexible than some rivals, and the machine struggled to build the same games catalogue or market momentum as the Spectrum and C64. Its Welsh manufacturing story remains an important part of Britain's computing history.

Oric-1 and Atmos: colour, sound and a better keyboard

Oric-1 · 1983

The Oric-1 was developed by Tangerine Computer Systems through Oric Products International. It used a 1 MHz 6502A, came in 16 KB and 48 KB versions, and included Oric Extended BASIC. Its graphics offered eight colours and its AY-3-8912 sound chip provided three programmable voices. At launch, the 16 KB model was priced at about £129.95 and the 48 KB model at £169.95.

Oric Atmos · 1984

The Atmos kept the Oric-1's basic architecture but replaced the rubber-key design with a much more conventional keyboard and updated the ROM to version 1.1. It was a meaningful improvement to the user experience, although compatibility quirks remained because some programs depended on details of the original ROM. Disk and printer peripherals eventually expanded the platform beyond cassette use.

A promising challenger

The Oric range had attractive specifications for its price, particularly its sound hardware, but reliability problems and a crowded market made life difficult. The story is a useful reminder that a good specification sheet was not enough: software support, distribution, dependable hardware and timing all mattered.

Apple II: an international family with a long life

Apple II · 1977

The Apple II helped establish the idea of a practical, expandable personal computer. Designed by Steve Wozniak, it used a 6502 processor and was notable for colour graphics, expansion slots and a design that could grow with its owner. It became a major platform for education, business software and games, particularly in North America.

Apple II Plus, IIe and later models

The family evolved through the Apple II Plus, IIe, IIc and IIgs. The IIe, introduced in 1983, refined the original platform and remained in production for years. The IIc made the design more compact and portable, while the IIgs later added more advanced graphics and sound. Across the family, expansion and a large software library helped preserve continuity.

Its place in the UK story

The Apple II was an American design rather than a British-built rival, but it belongs in this wider survey because it shaped the international personal-computer market and was encountered in the UK through specialist dealers, education and business. It provides a useful contrast with the lower-cost, television-connected British micros.

What these machines tell us

There was no single recipe for a successful 8-bit computer. Atari invested in custom silicon and a consistent peripheral system. Dragon paired an advanced processor with a more limited graphics design. Oric aimed for an attractive price and strong sound. Apple built an expandable platform that could evolve over many years. Their different fortunes were shaped as much by software, pricing, distribution and timing as by the hardware itself.

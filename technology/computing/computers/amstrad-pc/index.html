LEARNING/COMPUTING & TECHNOLOGY/CLASSIC COMPUTERS/AMSTRAD PC1512 & PC1640

16-BIT GENERATION / FAMILY 11

Amstrad PC1512 and PC1640:
the PC comes within reach.

Amstrad had already made a name with affordable home computers and hi-fi equipment. In 1986 it applied the same approach to IBM-compatible PCs. The PC1512 arrived as a complete package of computer, keyboard, mouse and monitor, at a price that brought the DOS software world within reach of more UK homes and small businesses. The PC1640 followed with more memory and improved display options.

FIRST MODELPC1512 · 1986
PROCESSORIntel 8086 · 8 MHz
MEMORY512 KB / 640 KB
OPERATING SYSTEMMS-DOS / DOS Plus

A complete PC package

The PC1512 was designed to be sold as a system rather than a bare computer. The monitor supplied power to the main unit, and the package included a keyboard and mouse. Configurations offered monochrome or colour displays, one or two floppy drives, and optional hard-disk versions. The PC1640 built on the same idea with 640 KB of RAM and a more capable graphics subsystem.

PC1512: the affordable entry point

Processor and memory

The PC1512 used an Intel 8086 running at 8 MHz and typically shipped with 512 KB of RAM, expandable to 640 KB. It was a 16-bit PC-compatible design, although its graphics and peripherals were more modest than those of more expensive business systems.

Graphics and sound

Its graphics adapter was CGA-compatible, with an additional 640 × 200 16-colour mode. The system used a simple internal speaker for sound. The colour monitor and the monochrome monitor offered very different experiences, and the software had to support the selected display mode.

Storage and expansion

Configurations included one or two 360 KB 5.25-inch floppy drives, with optional 10 or 20 MB hard disks. Three 8-bit ISA slots allowed some expansion, although the system was more integrated than a conventional modular tower PC.

Operating environment

MS-DOS 3.2 and DOS Plus were available, with GEM providing a graphical desktop option. The system could run the growing library of DOS business software and games, making it a practical first PC for many buyers.

PC1640: a step up

Introduced in 1987, the PC1640 retained the 8 MHz 8086 but increased memory to 640 KB and added an enhanced graphics adapter based on a Paradise chipset. It could support EGA-compatible colour graphics, while also accommodating other display modes and monitors. This made it more flexible for applications that benefited from higher-resolution or richer-colour displays.

More memory

The PC1640's 640 KB configuration made better use of the conventional DOS memory space without requiring an immediate memory upgrade.

Display flexibility

Its graphics hardware was the key differentiator. Depending on the monitor connected, the PC1640 could provide EGA, CGA and monochrome display compatibility, making it a more adaptable system than the PC1512.

Hard-disk variants

Some PC1640 configurations included a 20 MB hard disk, reducing the need to swap floppy disks and making the machine more suitable for regular office work.

Price and place in the UK market

The PC1512 launched in 1986 with advertised configurations from around £399 plus VAT for a monochrome single-drive system, and around £499 plus VAT for a colour version. The PC1640 sat higher in the range, with prices varying according to monitor and storage configuration. Those prices need to be read in context: the monitor, keyboard, mouse and bundled software made the package a comparatively accessible route into IBM-compatible computing.

What people used them for

  • Office work: word processing, spreadsheets, databases and business applications.
  • Education: DOS software and programming tools for schools and home learning.
  • Games: a growing catalogue of PC titles, though CGA graphics could look limited beside home computers such as the Amiga.
  • Home computing: a relatively affordable way to use the same broad software ecosystem as business PCs.

Strengths, compromises and legacy

The PC1512 and PC1640 made the PC experience more accessible through pricing and bundling. Their monitor-powered design and integrated layout were convenient, but less flexible than standardised modular systems. Graphics and sound were still limited by the era's PC standards, and the 8-bit ISA slots constrained some upgrades. The range helped establish Amstrad as a significant European PC maker and showed how the IBM-compatible market was moving beyond specialist business users.

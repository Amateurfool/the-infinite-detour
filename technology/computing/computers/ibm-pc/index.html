16-BIT GENERATION / FAMILY 10

The IBM PC:
an architecture becomes an industry.

IBM's Personal Computer, introduced in 1981, was not the first personal computer and did not invent every part of its design. Its importance came from a combination of IBM's reputation, a published technical architecture, widely available components and a software platform that other manufacturers could reproduce. The result was an ecosystem that grew far beyond IBM itself.

FIRST MODELIBM PC 5150 · 1981
PROCESSORIntel 8088 · 4.77 MHz
OPERATING SYSTEMPC DOS / MS-DOS
EXPANSION8-bit ISA bus

The machine that set a pattern

The 5150 was a desktop system with a separate keyboard and monitor. Its 8088 processor used an 8-bit external data bus, despite belonging to Intel's 16-bit 8086 family. Early configurations offered 16 KB of RAM, with expansion possible; the system supported floppy drives, optional cassette storage and internal expansion cards.

Inside the original PC

Intel 8088

The 8088 ran at 4.77 MHz and had a 16-bit internal architecture with an 8-bit external data bus. This helped IBM use a more economical support-chip design while retaining much of the 8086 software model.

Memory and expansion

The original 5150 shipped in several configurations, starting at 16 KB and supporting up to 256 KB on the motherboard. Five 8-bit expansion slots let owners add display adapters, memory, serial and parallel interfaces, disk controllers and other hardware.

Display options

IBM offered the Monochrome Display Adapter (MDA), designed for crisp text, and the Color Graphics Adapter (CGA), which supported colour graphics and basic sound output. The display adapter was a separate card, so a PC's capabilities depended on the card and monitor fitted.

PC DOS and BASIC

IBM supplied PC DOS, licensed from Microsoft, alongside a ROM BASIC option. The operating system and BIOS interface gave software developers a common target, while the published technical documentation encouraged compatible hardware.

From PC to XT and AT

IBM PC 5150 · 1981

The original PC established the architecture, with an open expansion bus and a design assembled from commercially available components. Its launch price began at about US$1,565 for a basic configuration.

IBM PC XT 5160 · 1983

The XT added a hard-disk option, more expansion capacity and a revised motherboard. It made the PC more practical for business users who needed faster access to larger amounts of data.

IBM PC AT 5170 · 1984

The AT moved to Intel's 80286 processor, a 16-bit external bus and a faster system architecture. It also brought a new keyboard interface and high-density floppy support, helping define the next tier of business PCs.

How compatibility spread

IBM published enough technical information for other companies to build compatible machines and expansion cards. Some manufacturers used clean-room reverse engineering to reproduce BIOS behaviour without copying IBM's code. Compaq's portable PC was an early landmark, and soon a wide range of companies offered IBM-compatible desktops. The term PC increasingly described an ecosystem rather than a single manufacturer's product.

The x86 family evolves

8086 and 8088

The 8086 and 8088 established the 16-bit x86 instruction set. The 8088's narrower external bus distinguished the original IBM PC from later systems built around the full 8086 bus.

80286 and 80386

The 286 introduced protected mode, while the 386 brought a 32-bit architecture and virtual 8086 mode. These processors expanded the capabilities of DOS PCs and helped pave the way for more sophisticated operating environments.

From DOS to Windows

MS-DOS and PC DOS were command-line operating systems, but graphical environments such as Windows grew in importance. As processors, memory and storage improved, the PC moved from a business-oriented machine into homes, schools, studios and games rooms.

What made the PC ecosystem powerful?

  • Compatibility: common hardware conventions and software expectations made it easier to move between manufacturers.
  • Expansion: users could add cards and peripherals rather than replace the whole system.
  • Competition: many manufacturers competed on price, performance and features.
  • Business software: spreadsheets, databases and word processors helped establish the PC in offices.
  • Games and creativity: graphics and sound cards gradually transformed the PC into a major home entertainment platform.

Strengths, compromises and legacy

The open, expandable architecture was the PC's great strength, but early systems could be complicated to configure and lacked the consistency of a tightly integrated computer. Graphics and sound varied by installed cards, and compatibility was not always perfect. Even so, the IBM PC architecture and x86 software ecosystem became the dominant foundation for personal computers for decades.

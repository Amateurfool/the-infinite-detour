Why do some fictional villains stay with us?

From Khan and Vader to the Master, the Joker, Saruman and Moriarty, explore what turns an antagonist into a character we never quite forget.

Some villains are frightening because they are powerful. Others are unsettling because they are persuasive, funny, wounded, brilliant or just a little too recognisable. The ones who stay with us tend to be more than obstacles for a hero to overcome. They have a presence of their own — a way of looking at the world that is difficult to forget, even when we would never want to live in it.

Consider six names from very different corners of popular fiction: Khan Noonien Singh, Darth Vader, the Master, the Joker, Saruman and Professor Moriarty. They are not interchangeable examples of evil. Each creates a different kind of dramatic pressure, and each reveals something about the story built around them.

A villain needs more than evil intentions.

A character who simply wants to destroy everything can be effective for a moment, but a memorable antagonist usually has a recognisable internal logic. They believe they are entitled to something, that the world has wronged them, that their methods are justified, or that everyone else is too weak or foolish to see the truth.

That logic does not have to be reasonable. It does have to feel like it belongs to the character. When we understand the shape of a villain's thinking, we can anticipate their choices without being able to predict exactly what they will do.

Khan Noonien Singh: pride with a personal grievance.

Khan is not memorable simply because he is physically formidable or intellectually superior. In Space Seed, he arrives with the confidence of a man who once ruled and expects the future to recognise his greatness. In Star Trek II: The Wrath of Khan, that pride has curdled into an intensely personal need for revenge. His world has collapsed, and he has fixed responsibility for that collapse on James Kirk.

That grievance gives Khan direction. He is theatrical, intelligent and capable of strategic patience, but his pride keeps pulling him towards a confrontation. He can understand a plan and still be unable to step outside the emotional logic driving it. Ricardo Montalbán's performance makes the contradiction vivid: Khan can sound cultured and controlled one moment, then burn with barely contained fury the next.

Later interpretations, including Star Trek Into Darkness, demonstrate how strongly a villain can be tied to a particular performance and context. Recasting the name is not automatically enough; the character's force comes from the combination of history, motivation and the presence brought to the screen.

The most dangerous weakness in a brilliant villain may be the one they mistake for strength.

Darth Vader: menace, mystery and the possibility of redemption.

When Darth Vader first enters Star Wars, he is almost pure cinematic authority. The breathing, the silhouette, the armour and the calm certainty with which he controls a room tell us what sort of power we are dealing with before we know his history. He is frightening partly because he seems so completely in command.

The original trilogy gradually changes the meaning of that image. Vader is not merely an imposing servant of the Empire; he is a father, a fallen Jedi and a person whose choices have devastated both himself and others. The prequel films add the long road to that fall, while later stories explore different parts of the same legacy. Not every addition carries the same weight, but the central tension remains: the monster was once someone who could have chosen differently.

Redemption does not erase what Vader has done. It gives the character a final choice that matters precisely because the damage is so great. That tension between accountability and the possibility of change is a large part of why he continues to resonate.

The Master: the villain who knows the hero too well.

The Master is one of Doctor Who's most flexible recurring antagonists because the conflict is personal before it is cosmic. The Doctor and the Master share a homeworld, extraordinary abilities and a long history. They are recognisable as counterparts, but their choices have sent them in radically different directions.

Across incarnations — from Roger Delgado's elegant, almost playful menace to Anthony Ainley's theatrical scheming, John Simm's volatile energy, Michelle Gomez's sharp-edged Missy and Sacha Dhawan's more openly destructive version — the character changes shape without losing that intimate rivalry. The Master can be an enemy, a reluctant ally, a friend who cannot quite be trusted or a distorted reflection of what the Doctor might have become.

That relationship creates unpredictability. The Master does not always want the same thing, and sometimes the desire to defeat the Doctor seems less important than the need to be understood by them. A recurring villain becomes richer when the history between adversaries is itself part of the story.

The Joker: chaos as a challenge to the hero's worldview.

The Joker is unusual because there is no single definitive interpretation that has to contain him. From comic books to animation, television and film, he has been a clownish criminal, a theatrical showman, a calculating terrorist, a grotesque trickster and a deeply unsettling study in obsession. The details shift, but the clash with Batman remains central.

Batman is defined by discipline, preparation and a personal code. The Joker attacks those foundations. He wants to prove that order is fragile, that morality is a thin disguise and that one terrible day can turn anyone into something like him. Different versions take that idea in different directions: some are flamboyant entertainers, others are coldly manipulative, and some are portrayed as painfully human.

The strongest interpretations make the Joker more than a collection of disturbing acts. They make him a question Batman cannot simply punch into silence: what does it mean to hold on to a principle when someone is determined to make that principle look foolish?

Saruman: the corruption of knowledge and authority.

Saruman begins with stature. He is not an outsider clawing for power but one of the wise, entrusted with knowledge and influence. His fall in The Lord of the Rings is therefore a particular kind of betrayal. He comes to believe that understanding the machinery of power gives him the right to use it, and that the ends can justify methods he would once have condemned.

His voice, robes, tower and carefully ordered industrial machinery all reinforce the same idea: intelligence has become arrogance, and order has become domination. Saruman is dangerous because he can make surrender sound practical. He offers a version of the future in which resistance is childish and compromise with evil is merely realism.

That makes him a useful counterpoint to Sauron. Saruman is not simply a distant force of darkness; he is a respected figure whose judgement has been corrupted from within. His fall asks how easily expertise, status and good intentions can become excuses for control.

Moriarty: an opponent on the hero's own level.

Professor Moriarty is often more powerful as an idea than as a fully present character. In Arthur Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes stories, he is introduced as the intellectual force behind a vast criminal network — the rare opponent who can meet Holmes on the level of abstraction, planning and deduction where the detective feels most secure.

That is the essential trick. Moriarty threatens Holmes not only with violence but with the possibility of being outthought. Later adaptations have made him more visible, more theatrical, more physically dangerous or more emotionally entangled with Holmes. Some reinvent the details almost entirely. The enduring appeal is the same: the detective needs an adversary who can make his greatest strength feel insufficient.

The idea has travelled beyond Holmes adaptations, too. Star Trek: The Next Generation used a holographic Moriarty as an unexpected challenge for Data, turning the familiar premise into a question about consciousness and what it means to be more than a programmed character. The name works because it carries an expectation: this is someone who might actually beat the genius at his own game.

So, what makes a villain last?

These six characters endure for different reasons. Khan is driven by wounded pride. Vader combines terrifying authority with a tragic history. The Master makes rivalry intimate. The Joker attacks the hero's moral foundations. Saruman shows the corruption of wisdom. Moriarty makes intelligence itself a battlefield.

There is no single formula. A villain can be memorable through performance, design, motivation, mystery, a powerful relationship with the hero or an idea that keeps troubling us after the story ends. Often the best antagonists combine several of these qualities.

Perhaps the most revealing test is this: remove the hero for a moment and ask whether the villain still feels like a person with a history, a worldview and a future they are trying to force into existence. If the answer is yes, the character has probably become more than a plot device.

Motivation

What does the antagonist believe they deserve, and what justifies their actions in their own mind?

Relationship

Why is this particular hero the person who can challenge or expose them?

Presence

What do voice, design, behaviour and performance communicate before the character explains themselves?

Contradiction

What weakness, fear or blind spot undermines the strength the villain relies on?

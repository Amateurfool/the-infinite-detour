Why do we keep imagining the future as a city?

From towering skylines to the ordinary lives unfolding beneath them, discover why the imagined city is such a powerful way to explore tomorrow.

FEATURE / IMAGINED WORLDS7 MIN READEVERGREEN GUIDE

On this detour

  1. The city as a character
  2. Exaggerating the present
  3. Technology and daily life
  4. The ordinary beside the extraordinary
  5. Reading a fictional city

The future is often introduced with a skyline. Towers climb into the clouds, aircraft thread between buildings, enormous screens compete for attention and the streets below are crowded with people trying to get through an ordinary day. It is a familiar image, but it tells us something important: when we imagine tomorrow, we often start by rearranging the world we already know.

The city as a character.

A convincing speculative city is more than an impressive collection of buildings. Its layout tells a story about who holds power, who has money, who is watched and who gets left behind. A corporate district elevated above the streets suggests a different society from a city where public space is genuinely shared.

Look at the routes people take. Where do they live in relation to work? Who controls transport, water, energy and communication? Which neighbourhoods are celebrated, and which are hidden from visitors? These details make a fictional place feel inhabited rather than merely designed.

The most believable future city is not the one with the most technology. It is the one where you can imagine having to live.

Exaggerating the present.

Cyberpunk often feels futuristic because it turns existing tensions up to an extraordinary volume. Advertising becomes architecture. Data becomes a form of ownership. Work follows people everywhere. The gap between those who can buy protection and those who cannot becomes part of the landscape.

This is why a story can feel prophetic without accurately predicting a particular invention. It recognises a pressure already building in society and asks what happens if that pressure is allowed to grow unchecked. The technology supplies the visual language; the social question gives it meaning.

Technology should change daily life.

Futuristic devices are most interesting when they alter ordinary behaviour. If everyone has an augmented display, how do people pay attention to one another? If machines perform most routine work, who owns them and how is wealth distributed? If a person can change their body, voice or memories, what happens to identity and trust?

A useful test for any imagined technology is to follow its consequences beyond the moment of invention. Consider its cost, maintenance, failure modes, unintended uses and the people who cannot access it. A world becomes richer when technology creates new problems as well as solving old ones.

The ordinary beside the extraordinary.

Grand vistas establish scale, but small human details create attachment. A late train, a crowded food stall, a broken lift or a familiar argument can make a distant future feel close. The contrast is powerful: the setting may be astonishing, but the people still have rent to pay, friends to disappoint and somewhere they would rather be.

Fantasy worlds benefit from the same principle. A kingdom feels more real when we glimpse its markets, roads, kitchens and local customs, not only its royal court and ancient prophecy. The everyday is not a distraction from the spectacle. It is what gives the spectacle somewhere to land.

A practical way to read a fictional city.

Next time a story introduces a futuristic city, look beyond the skyline. Ask who designed it, who benefits from its systems, what has been pushed out of sight and what a normal working day looks like for someone with no special importance to the plot.

Then imagine one small disruption: the transport network stops, the power fails or the central data system goes offline. Which people are affected first? Who has a workaround? The answers reveal the structure of the fictional society far more clearly than another sweeping aerial shot.

Random places: Chester, Plymouth and Portmeirion

A personal collection of places, beginning with three destinations that could hardly be more different: a Roman city, a maritime port and an architectural village.

Not every place needs to fit a category. Some destinations are famous landmarks; others are towns, oddities or places that simply stay in the memory. This collection begins with factual introductions to three places: Chester, Plymouth and Portmeirion. Later, it can become a more personal record, with your own photographs, recollections and reasons for including them.

Chester: Roman foundations and medieval streets

Chester stands beside the River Dee in north-west England, close to the Welsh border. Its origins lie in the Roman fortress of Deva Victrix, established in the 1st century AD. The modern street plan still follows the lines of several principal Roman roads, and the remains of the Roman amphitheatre survive close to the city centre.

The city is particularly known for its walls and the Rows. The walls form one of the most complete surviving circuits of Roman and medieval city defences in Britain. The Rows are a distinctive two-level arrangement of shops, with covered walkways at first-floor level running along several central streets.

Chester’s familiar black-and-white streetscape can look much older than it is: many of the buildings that give the centre its character are Victorian interpretations of earlier timber-framed architecture. The result is a city where Roman planning, medieval structures and Victorian reinvention sit close together.

Plymouth: a city shaped by the sea

Plymouth sits on the south coast of Devon, where the Rivers Plym and Tamar meet Plymouth Sound, a large natural harbour. Its maritime position shaped the city’s growth as a centre for fishing, trade, shipbuilding and naval activity.

Plymouth Hoe is one of the city’s best-known open spaces, looking across the Sound. Its landmarks include the statue of Sir Francis Drake and Smeaton’s Tower. The lighthouse was originally built on the Eddystone Reef and, after a replacement was constructed offshore, the upper part was dismantled and re-erected on the Hoe in the 1880s.

The city is also closely associated with the Mayflower story: the ship’s 1620 voyage to North America began from Plymouth. Around the historic Barbican and waterfront, the city’s connections with seafaring, exploration and naval history remain visible.

Portmeirion: an Italianate village in North Wales

Portmeirion is a planned village on a private peninsula on the coast of North Wales, near Porthmadog. Its colourful buildings, piazzas, gardens and coastal setting give it a distinctly Mediterranean appearance, despite its Welsh location.

The village was created by architect Sir Clough Williams-Ellis, who acquired the site in 1925 and developed it over several decades. His aim was to demonstrate how architecture could complement a beautiful landscape rather than overwhelm it. The village was completed in 1976.

Portmeirion is also widely recognised as the filming location for the 1960s television series The Prisoner. Its mixture of architectural styles, carefully composed views and unusual setting makes it an architectural experiment as much as a visitor attraction.

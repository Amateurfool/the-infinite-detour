Does an adaptation need to be faithful to its source?

From The Martian and Ready Player One to Tolkien, Blade Runner, Sherlock Holmes and James Bond, explore what faithfulness really means when a story changes medium.

A book becomes a film. A television series reimagines a famous detective. A much-loved science-fiction novel is turned into a cinematic spectacle. Almost immediately, the comparison begins: what was changed, what was left out, what was misunderstood — and was the adaptation faithful?

It sounds like a straightforward question, but it hides another one: faithful to what? A story is not just a sequence of events. It is also a point of view, a mood, a set of ideas, a rhythm and a relationship with its audience. Moving it to another medium means deciding which of those things matter most.

First, what do we mean by faithful?

Sometimes faithfulness means preserving the plot. Sometimes it means keeping characters recognisable, retaining the themes or reproducing the atmosphere. It might mean making the audience feel something similar, even if the route to that feeling is completely different.

These aims can conflict. A novel can spend pages inside a character's thoughts; a film has to find a visual or dramatic way to communicate the same information. A television series can take its time with a supporting character; a two-hour film may have to combine roles or remove them. A game can let the audience make choices that a novel's author deliberately fixed.

So a useful question is not simply whether an adaptation changes things. It is whether the changes serve the new work — and whether they preserve something essential about the original.

The Martian: close to the book, but not a duplicate.

Andy Weir's The Martian is a good example of a relatively close adaptation. The central predicament remains intact: astronaut Mark Watney is stranded on Mars and has to use practical knowledge, improvisation and stubborn determination to stay alive while people on Earth work out how to bring him home.

The novel's appeal comes partly from its problem-solving detail and Watney's first-person voice. A reader gets close to the calculations, failed experiments, small victories and gallows humour. A film cannot simply reproduce pages of technical reasoning without becoming static, so Ridley Scott's version turns much of that process into images, performance, editing and scenes of collaboration on Earth.

There are compressions and changes, and some of the book's technical explanation is simplified. Yet the adaptation preserves the central pleasure: watching a resourceful person turn an impossible situation into a series of solvable problems. It is faithful less because every incident survives than because the film understands what makes the novel satisfying.

Ready Player One: when the screen needs a different game.

Ernest Cline's Ready Player One is built around a protagonist moving through a virtual world packed with references, puzzles and cultural history. The novel's challenges often depend on the reader recognising the material and following the logic of a hunt across a very large collection of pop-culture clues.

Steven Spielberg's film keeps the broad premise and the central race for control of the OASIS, but it substantially reworks the challenges, references, character moments and action. Some of the book's set pieces are replaced, rearranged or transformed. That is not a minor adjustment: the film is playing a different version of the game.

There are practical reasons. Screen rights, audience recognition, pacing and visual clarity all shape what can appear in a major film. But there is also a creative question: should the film reproduce the book's exact scavenger hunt, or create a cinematic adventure that communicates the same excitement of discovery? Whether the result works is a separate judgement from whether it is faithful. The film is best approached as a reinterpretation of the premise, not a page-by-page transfer.

The Lord of the Rings: preserving the journey while changing the route.

Peter Jackson's The Lord of the Rings films make substantial changes to J.R.R. Tolkien's novels. Events are compressed, timelines are tightened, characters and episodes are omitted or combined, and some personalities are emphasised differently. The books' narrative voice, songs, histories and long stretches of reflection cannot all fit into a theatrical film trilogy.

Some changes are structural necessities; others are creative choices that remain debated by readers. Yet the films retain the central journey, the moral weight of the Ring, the importance of friendship and endurance, and the sense that ordinary people can carry responsibilities far beyond their own strength. They translate the scale and emotional architecture of the books into a different form.

That does not mean every change is automatically justified. A character alteration can shift the meaning of a scene; an omitted episode can remove a piece of the world's texture. But the trilogy demonstrates that fidelity can exist at the level of theme and emotional experience even when the route through the plot is altered.

Blade Runner: an adaptation that becomes its own world.

Blade Runner takes its starting point from Philip K. Dick's Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?, but the film is not a close reproduction. It changes names, emphasis, events and the texture of the world. The novel's concerns with empathy, artificial life and the uncertain boundary between human and android remain part of the conversation, but the film develops them through its own visual language and atmosphere.

Rain-slick streets, monumental architecture, crowded interiors, synthetic animals and Vangelis's music create a world that feels dense and melancholy. The film's questions are often left unresolved or made deliberately ambiguous. Its identity is inseparable from the way it looks and sounds — qualities that a faithful transcription of the novel's scenes would not necessarily have produced.

This is a case where distance from the source helps create a distinct work. The film invites viewers back to Dick's ideas, but it does not need to behave like the novel to be a meaningful adaptation of it.

Sherlock Holmes: how much can you change before the character changes?

Sherlock Holmes may be one of fiction's most adaptable characters because the core ingredients are so strong: extraordinary observation, deductive reasoning, an unusual personality and a partnership with Dr Watson. Those elements can survive very different settings and styles.

Some adaptations aim for period atmosphere and a close relationship with Conan Doyle's stories. Others make Holmes more physically active, more emotionally exposed, more eccentric or more contemporary. The BBC series Sherlock relocates the detective to modern London; Guy Ritchie's films turn the Victorian setting into kinetic action-adventure; other versions return to the original period and emphasise mystery and deduction.

Each version makes choices about what is essential. If Holmes becomes simply a generic action hero, something distinctive may be lost. But a new setting or personality emphasis can reveal a different aspect of the character. Faithfulness here is less about freezing Holmes in one definitive form and more about retaining the qualities that make the detective recognisable.

James Bond: a character designed to change with the times.

The Bond films have always treated Ian Fleming's novels as source material rather than an unbreakable blueprint. The films borrow characters, situations, titles and ideas, but the series has repeatedly adjusted tone, technology, humour, violence and the portrayal of Bond himself to fit changing audiences.

Sean Connery's Bond, Roger Moore's lighter interpretation, Timothy Dalton's harder-edged agent, Pierce Brosnan's polished action hero and Daniel Craig's more emotionally exposed version do not feel identical. Nor should they. The franchise's continuity is selective: it preserves the central figure, the espionage setting, the rituals and recognisable motifs, while repeatedly rebuilding the world around them.

That flexibility can be a strength, but it also raises a challenge. If every era reshapes Bond, what must remain for the character to still be Bond? The answer is not one fixed personality trait. It is a balance of competence, danger, charm, vulnerability and the particular fantasy of the secret agent that each film chooses to present.

So, does an adaptation need to be faithful?

It needs to be faithful to something — but that something does not always have to be the plot. The Martian preserves much of its source's structure and problem-solving spirit. Ready Player One reshapes its puzzles for a different kind of spectacle. The Lord of the Rings compresses a vast narrative while retaining much of its emotional and thematic core. Blade Runner uses a novel as a springboard for a distinctive cinematic vision. Sherlock Holmes and James Bond show how characters can survive repeated reinvention when the right essentials remain.

There is no universal rule that the book is always better, or that a film should be free to change anything it likes. A change should be judged by what it achieves. Does it make the story clearer in the new medium? Does it deepen a character, sharpen a theme or create an experience that could only work on screen? Or does it remove something important without replacing it with anything meaningful?

Perhaps the best adaptation is not the one that copies the most. It is the one that understands what made the original worth adapting — and then finds an honest, imaginative way to make that matter in a new form.

Plot

Which events are essential, and which can be compressed or replaced without damaging the story?

Character

Do the people still feel recognisable, even when their circumstances or behaviour change?

Spirit

Does the adaptation preserve the themes, atmosphere or emotional effect that made the source distinctive?

Medium

Does it use the strengths of film, television or another format rather than merely illustrating the source?

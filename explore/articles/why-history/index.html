Why are we fascinated by history and historical locations?

Castles, ancient theatres and legendary headlands offer more than old stones. They let us stand inside landscapes shaped by people, power, belief and time.

A historical location is more than a collection of old buildings. It is a point where the past becomes tangible: a defensive wall can reveal what people feared, a theatre can show how a community gathered, and a remote headland can carry stories that have travelled through centuries. Dover Castle, Tintagel Castle and Aspendos are very different places, but each offers a way into history.

Dover Castle: the key to England

Dover Castle occupies a commanding position above the harbour and the narrowest part of the English Channel. Its strategic setting made it one of the country’s most important defensive sites, earning it the familiar description ‘the key to England’.

The hill was used long before the medieval castle took shape. The Romans built a lighthouse here, parts of which survive beside the later church of St Mary in Castro. The castle visible today was largely established under Henry II between 1179 and 1189, and its defences were tested during the sieges of 1216 and 1217.

Its history did not stop in the Middle Ages. The network of tunnels was adapted for military use during the Napoleonic period and again during the Second World War, when the underground command centre played a role in the planning of the Dunkirk evacuation. The site therefore brings together Roman, medieval and modern military history.

Tintagel Castle: where history meets legend

Tintagel stands on a dramatic headland on Cornwall’s north coast, with the remains of the castle spread across the mainland and the rocky island. The setting is inseparable from its reputation, but the site has a substantial history beyond its association with King Arthur.

From around the 5th to 7th centuries, Tintagel was an important stronghold and trading centre. Archaeological finds, including imported pottery from the Mediterranean, point to connections reaching well beyond Cornwall. In the 13th century Richard, Earl of Cornwall, built a medieval castle here; most of the visible castle remains date from this phase.

The Arthurian connection grew through medieval literature, particularly Geoffrey of Monmouth’s account of Arthur’s conception at Tintagel. It is a powerful part of the place’s identity, but it belongs to the history of the legend rather than proof that the historical King Arthur lived here. Today a footbridge links the two sides of the site.

Aspendos: a Roman theatre built to last

Aspendos lies inland from Antalya, near the modern town of Serik in southern Türkiye. The ancient city contains the remains of a wider settlement, but its best-known monument is the remarkably well-preserved Roman theatre.

The theatre was built in the 2nd century AD, during the reign of the emperor Marcus Aurelius, and is traditionally attributed to the architect Zenon. Its tiered auditorium and monumental stage building survive in exceptional condition. Official Turkish tourism sources describe a capacity of around 15,000 spectators, although estimates vary between sources.

The theatre is only part of the archaeological site. Visitors can also find remains of the agora, basilica, monumental fountain, city walls and an extensive aqueduct system that brought water from the surrounding hills. The theatre’s later use and restoration during the Seljuk period helped preserve this extraordinary example of Roman engineering.

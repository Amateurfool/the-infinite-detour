8-BIT GENERATION / FAMILY 02

Commodore:
computing at scale.

Commodore took a different route to Sinclair. It built and sold machines across business, education and home markets, and its ownership of key semiconductor manufacturing helped it compete on price. The result was a family of computers that ranged from office-ready PET systems to the VIC-20, the C64 phenomenon and the unusually ambitious C128.

From office machine to living-room computer

PET · 1977

The Personal Electronic Transactor arrived as an integrated system with a built-in monitor and keyboard. The original PET 2001 included a compact keyboard and cassette storage; later PET and CBM models expanded memory, improved keyboards and supported floppy disks. PETs became familiar in schools, offices and computer rooms.

VIC-20 · 1980/81

First introduced in Japan as the VIC-1001 in 1980, then released in Europe and North America in 1981, the VIC-20 was a relatively affordable colour home computer. Its MOS 6502 processor, VIC graphics chip and sound capabilities offered a friendly entry point, although the standard 5 KB of RAM left little room for large programs.

Commodore 64 · 1982

The C64 combined a MOS 6510 processor, 64 KB of RAM, the VIC-II graphics chip and the SID sound synthesiser. Its colour graphics and distinctive three-voice sound made it a powerful games and music platform. It also supported serious programming, education and productivity software.

Commodore 128 · 1985

The C128 added 128 KB of RAM, an 80-column display option and a Z80 processor for CP/M compatibility, while retaining a C64 mode for the enormous existing software library. Its dual-processor design and multiple operating modes made it one of the most technically interesting 8-bit machines.

The C64 was a family, not a single beige box

The original breadbin-shaped C64 was revised during its life. The C64C introduced a slimmer case and updated board design. The SX-64 was a portable, suitcase-style C64 with a built-in colour screen. The Educator 64 used a PET-style monitor case for institutional markets. There were also regional and board revisions, special editions and later reissues. These variants differed in appearance and practical details, but remained part of the wider C64 ecosystem.

Other Commodore 8-bit machines

The PET, VIC-20, C64 and C128 were not the whole story. The later Plus/4 and C16 formed another branch, with a different chipset and built-in productivity software on the Plus/4. The C128 also appeared as the C128D, with an integrated disk drive and, in some versions, a metal case and separate keyboard. Earlier PET/CBM ranges had numerous memory and keyboard variants, reflecting their long life in education and business.

Inside the machine: why the C64 mattered

  • Memory: 64 KB was generous for a home computer of its launch period, though the CPU's address space and memory banking required careful programming.
  • Graphics: the VIC-II supported hardware sprites, smooth scrolling features and a range of bitmap and character modes.
  • Sound: the SID chip offered three programmable voices, filters and modulation capabilities that became central to the C64's musical identity.
  • Storage: cassette drives were common, while the 1541 disk drive offered more convenient access but was relatively expensive and had unusual design characteristics.

What was it like to own one?

For many owners, the computer was a keyboard connected to a family television, with programs loaded from cassette and later from floppy disk. A huge catalogue of games made the C64 a familiar living-room presence, but it was also a machine people learned to program, composed music on and expanded with printers, modems, joysticks and disk drives.

Legacy

The C64's longevity came from the combination of strong hardware, competitive pricing, a vast software catalogue and an enthusiastic community. The C128 showed how far the 8-bit platform could be extended, while preserving a practical route back to the C64 library. Commodore's story also connects directly to the Amiga, which became its next major chapter in home computing.

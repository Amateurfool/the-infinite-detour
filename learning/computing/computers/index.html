COMPUTING / PROFILE COLLECTION 01

A generation
of ideas.

A guided journey through the home computers that changed everyday life. We begin with the inventive, varied 8-bit era — from familiar British favourites to machines made around the world — then move into the 16-bit generation and the more powerful systems that followed.

01 / THE 8-BIT GENERATION

When a computer came home.

Many families met computing through these machines. They were gateways to programming, education, games, music and a new kind of creative hobby.

FAMILY 01 / SINCLAIR

Sinclair: ZX80 to Spectrum

From the ZX80 and ZX81 to the many Spectrum generations and variants, with the QL as a bridge to a different era.

Commodore: PET to C128

The PET, VIC-20, C64 family and its variants, plus the ambitious C128 and C128D.

Explore the Commodore family ↗ FAMILY 03 / ACORN

Acorn: Atom to BBC Master

The Atom, Electron, BBC Micro models and Master family — education, expansion and British engineering.

FAMILY 04 / OTHER 8-BIT MACHINESPLANNED

Beyond the usual suspects

Atari 400, 800 and XL computers; Dragon 32 and 64; Oric-1 and Atmos; the Apple II family; and other machines that could be found in British homes, schools and shops.

Late 1970s–1980s · A diverse 8-bit landscape
FAMILY 05 / MSXPLANNED

One standard, many makers

MSX was a shared computer standard adopted by multiple manufacturers. Explore the different machines and the evolution from MSX to MSX2, MSX2+ and Turbo R, including what compatibility did — and did not — mean.

1983 onward · Multi-manufacturer Z80 standard
FAMILY 06 / AMSTRADPLANNED

Amstrad CPC: 464 to 6128

A major part of the British 8-bit story: the CPC464, CPC664 and CPC6128, their integrated designs, colour graphics, sound and distinctive place in the European market.

1984 onward · Z80-based 8-bit family

02 / THE 16-BIT GENERATION

More power, new possibilities.

Richer graphics, larger memories, multitasking and new creative tools. The move to 16-bit computing was not one single path: different manufacturers took it in very different directions.

FAMILY 07 / COMMODOREPLANNED

Commodore Amiga

From the original A1000 to the A500, A2000, A600 and A1200, exploring the custom chips, graphics, sound, Workbench and the machine's influence on games and creative media.

1985 onward · 68000-based 16/32-bit family
FAMILY 08 / ATARIPLANNED

Atari ST

The 520ST and 1040ST, later STE models and the Falcon: a family remembered for its value, graphical interface, games and especially its role in affordable MIDI music production.

1985 onward · 68000-based 16/32-bit family
FAMILY 09 / APPLEPLANNED

Apple Macintosh

From the compact original Macintosh to later models, exploring the graphical desktop, mouse-driven interaction, publishing and design, and the transition through the 68000 family.

1984 onward · 68000-era personal computers
FAMILY 10 / IBM & COMPATIBLESPLANNED

The IBM PC and its descendants

The original IBM PC and XT, the AT and the expanding world of compatible machines: how an open ecosystem helped establish the PC as a lasting business and home platform.

1981 onward · 8088/8086 and x86 evolution
FAMILY 11 / AMSTRADPLANNED

Amstrad PC1512 and PC1640

Amstrad's move into IBM-compatible personal computers, bringing a comparatively affordable PC package to a wider UK home and small-business market.

1986 onward · 16-bit PC-compatible systems

03 / BEYOND THE 16-BIT LABEL

Bridges and new architectures.

Some important machines do not fit neatly into a simple 8-bit or 16-bit story. Their architecture deserves a little explanation rather than an inaccurate label.

TRANSITION / SINCLAIRPLANNED

Sinclair QL

A distinctive 68008-based machine with a 32-bit internal architecture and 16-bit external data bus: a fascinating step away from the familiar 8-bit micros.

1984 · 68008 architecture
NEXT GENERATION / ACORNPLANNED

Acorn Archimedes

The ARM-based Archimedes family and RISC OS: a British design with a different approach to processor architecture, performance and graphical computing.

1987 onward · 32-bit ARM systems

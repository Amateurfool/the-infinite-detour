LEARNING/COMPUTING & TECHNOLOGY/CLASSIC COMPUTERS/AMSTRAD CPC

8-BIT GENERATION / FAMILY 06

A computer of
its own.

Amstrad arrived in the home-computer market later than Sinclair and Commodore, but it had a clear idea about what a family should buy: a complete computer, with a proper keyboard, built-in storage and a dedicated monitor. The CPC range turned that straightforward proposition into one of Britain's defining 8-bit platforms.

ERA1984–1990
PROCESSORZilog Z80A · 4 MHz
MEMORY64 KB / 128 KB RAM
STORAGECassette or 3-inch disk

The CPC idea: a complete setup

The CPC name stood for Colour Personal Computer. Unlike many rivals, the original CPC package was designed around a dedicated monitor rather than a television. The computer itself was the keyboard unit, with a cassette deck built into the CPC464 or a disk drive in the CPC664 and CPC6128. The monitor supplied power to the computer, so the system was intended to be simple to set up: monitor, keyboard unit, and away you went.

CPC464: the original all-in-one · 1984

Hardware and design

The CPC464 paired a Zilog Z80A processor running at 4 MHz with 64 KB of RAM, 16 KB of dedicated video memory and a built-in cassette deck. Amstrad’s advertised complete-system prices were £199 with the green monitor and £299 with the colour monitor. Its full-sized keyboard had coloured function keys, and the computer was normally sold with either a green-screen monitor or a colour monitor. The monitor contained the power supply for the keyboard unit, a detail that helped make the package feel like one integrated product.

Graphics and sound

The CPC's Gate Array and video circuitry offered three main screen modes: 160 × 200 pixels with up to 16 colours, 320 × 200 with four colours, or 640 × 200 with two colours. The palette contained 27 possible colours. Sound came from the General Instrument AY-3-8912, with three tone channels and a noise generator. These capabilities gave developers a useful balance between colourful games and sharper text or graphics.

Locomotive BASIC

Locomotive BASIC was one of the CPC's strengths. It included commands for graphics, sound, sprites-like user-defined characters and access to the machine's hardware. The CPC464 shipped with BASIC 1.0; later disk models used BASIC 1.1. The combination of a substantial keyboard, monitor and capable built-in language made the CPC a practical machine for learning as well as playing.

CPC664: cassette gives way to disk · 1985

A short-lived middle model

Introduced in spring 1985, the CPC664 replaced the built-in cassette deck with a 3-inch floppy-disk drive while retaining 64 KB of RAM. UK launch prices were £339 with a green-screen monitor and £449 with a colour monitor. Its case and keyboard were redesigned, with a more restrained grey-and-blue key scheme. It also introduced Locomotive BASIC 1.1 and included the AMSDOS disk operating system.

Why the disk mattered

Disk storage made loading and saving programs more convenient and supported a more serious range of applications. The CPC664 was also promoted as an affordable route into CP/M 2.2. Its disk drive required a 12-volt supply, which was provided through the updated monitor arrangements. The drive used the unusual 3-inch disk format rather than the 3.5-inch format that became more common elsewhere.

Superseded quickly

The CPC664 was on sale for only around six months before the CPC6128 arrived. It was not a failure so much as a brief stepping stone: Amstrad had learned that disk users also wanted more memory, and the next model addressed both needs in one machine.

CPC6128: more memory, more ambition · 1985

128 KB in the same family

The CPC6128 doubled the main memory to 128 KB and retained the integrated 3-inch disk drive. UK launch pricing was reported at £299 with a green-screen monitor and £399 with a colour monitor. It used the same Z80A at 4 MHz and remained closely related to the earlier CPCs, so the existing software library was a major part of its appeal. The extra memory was bank-switched rather than appearing as one simple, contiguous 128 KB block to every program.

CP/M and business software

The larger memory made the CPC6128 a more comfortable CP/M machine. It could run CP/M 2.2 and CP/M Plus (3.0), opening access to business and productivity software beyond the CPC's games catalogue. It was still a home computer at heart, but it could plausibly serve as a small office or study machine too.

A European success

The CPC range found particular success in the UK, France, Spain and German-speaking markets, where Schneider sold rebadged versions in Germany. Its integrated package, strong BASIC and broad software library gave it a distinct position between the low-cost TV-connected micros and more expensive business systems.

What made the CPC distinctive?

  • The monitor was part of the proposition: the bundled dedicated display freed the family television and made the system feel self-contained.
  • Storage was built in: a cassette deck in the 464, a 3-inch disk drive in the 664 and 6128.
  • A flexible video system: three screen modes traded resolution against colour depth, with a 27-colour palette.
  • Strong sound for the period: the AY-3-8912 became familiar across many 8-bit machines.
  • Useful software beyond games: Locomotive BASIC, AMSDOS and CP/M gave the range a wider role.

464 Plus, 6128 Plus and GX4000 · 1990

A late refresh

In 1990 Amstrad refreshed the range with the 464 Plus and 6128 Plus. These machines retained the broad CPC identity but added enhanced graphics hardware, a cartridge slot and a redesigned case. The built-in monitor concept was dropped: the keyboard unit was again sold with a separate monitor, which supplied power.

From computer to console

The GX4000 used closely related Plus hardware in a dedicated games-console format. It offered cartridge games and a more console-like design, but arrived into a market dominated by established Japanese systems. The GX4000 and Plus computers were technically interesting, but they came too late to restore the CPC's earlier momentum.

The end of the line

The Plus generation is a useful coda to the CPC story: Amstrad tried to extend a successful 8-bit platform into a new decade, but the wider market was moving towards 16-bit computers and newer consoles. The original CPC range had already secured its place in European home-computing history.

Legacy

The Amstrad CPC was not the first British home computer, nor the cheapest, nor the most technically exotic. Its success came from understanding the whole experience: a proper keyboard, dedicated monitor, storage built into the machine, a capable BASIC and a clear retail package. It helped make computing feel less like a collection of separate components and more like something a household could simply bring home and use.

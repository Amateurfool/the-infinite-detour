COMPUTING HISTORY / THE TOOLS WE USE

Software: from useful programs to digital life

Software is where the computer becomes a tool. This history follows the applications that changed how people write, calculate, organise information, create images and work together.

Early programs: doing one job well

Early home computers often arrived with BASIC built in, and many users learned by typing programs from books and magazines. Other software was loaded from cassette or disk. The distinction between a computer and its software was still becoming familiar: a machine's usefulness depended heavily on the programs available for it.

Word processing: the typewriter changes

Word processors transformed writing by making it possible to edit, move and reformat text without retyping an entire page. Programs such as WordPerfect became important in offices, while home users encountered a growing range of word-processing packages. Spell-checking, formatting, templates and printing gradually became expected features. The early interfaces could look intimidating: keyboard commands and function-key menus were often quicker than navigating a mouse-driven interface once you knew them.

Spreadsheets: the number-crunching revolution

Electronic spreadsheets made calculations and financial modelling interactive. Change one value and dependent totals update automatically. Lotus 1-2-3 became a major business application in the 1980s, followed by Microsoft Excel and other spreadsheet tools. For many organisations, spreadsheets changed how budgets, forecasts and data were handled. A grid of cells became a flexible modelling tool: formulas, recalculation, charts and later macros turned it into a kind of programmable workspace.

Databases and organising information

Database software moved beyond specialist business systems and into small offices and homes. Users could store structured records, search them, sort them and produce reports. Desktop database tools helped clubs, small businesses and enthusiasts manage information without building a bespoke system.

Desktop publishing and creative software

As graphical interfaces, printers and displays improved, personal computers became tools for design and publishing. Desktop publishing brought page layout into offices and homes. Image editors, drawing packages, music sequencers and video tools later expanded the creative possibilities. A home computer could become a recording studio, photo lab, typesetting desk or video-editing workstation — provided it had the right software and peripherals.

The office suite era

Applications increasingly arrived as suites, sharing menus, file formats and a common visual language. Word processors, spreadsheets, presentations and email became part of a familiar office toolkit. Compatibility mattered: documents had to move between colleagues, organisations and different generations of software. Today, open formats and cross-platform suites sit alongside proprietary ecosystems, and a document may be edited locally, in a browser or collaboratively in real time.

The web changes the software model

Web browsers became platforms for applications. Email, documents, project management, shopping and media moved online. Software no longer always needed to be installed from a disc; it could be delivered through a browser and updated centrally.

Subscriptions, collaboration and AI-assisted tools

Modern software often combines local applications with cloud accounts and shared storage. Several people can edit the same document, collaborate across locations and access their work on different devices. Software is increasingly sold as a service, with continuous updates replacing the old cycle of buying a boxed upgrade every few years. More recently, AI features have appeared in writing, coding, design, search and productivity tools — raising new questions about trust, authorship, privacy and the role of human judgement.

What changed beneath the surface?

INTERFACE

From typed commands and keyboard shortcuts to graphical toolbars, touch and voice.

FILES

From program-specific documents on floppy disks to shared formats, synchronisation and cloud storage.

COLLABORATION

From passing a disk or printed copy around to live co-editing and shared workspaces.

BUSINESS MODEL

From one-off boxed purchases to downloads, subscriptions and browser-based services.

A useful way to explore software history

Choose a task — writing a letter, balancing a household budget or editing a photograph — and compare how you would have done it in 1985, 1995, 2005 and today. The changes in software reveal just as much about the culture of computing as the hardware does.
